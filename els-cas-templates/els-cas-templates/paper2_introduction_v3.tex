\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The deep integration of physical infrastructure with high-speed
communication networks has transformed traditional power grids into
\emph{cyber-physical power systems} (CPPS), in which a physical power
layer and a cyber communication layer operate as a tightly coupled
whole \citep{Rinaldi2001,Ouyang2014}.  Under nominal conditions this
coupling improves dispatch flexibility, situational awareness and
wide-area control performance.  Under stress, however, it opens a
bidirectional failure channel: a disturbance originating in either
layer can propagate to the other and trigger cascading failures of a
magnitude neither layer would experience in isolation
\citep{Buldyrev2010,Gao2012}.

A substantial body of work has investigated such failures through
topological percolation models, flow-redistribution rules and
attack-vulnerability analyses
\citep{Motter2002,Dobson2007,Brummitt2012,Schneider2011}.  These
studies established \emph{how} structural interdependence amplifies
failure propagation, and they remain the conceptual backbone of
modern CPPS robustness analysis.  In most of them, however, the
communication layer is represented as a binary entity---a link is
either functional or failed---without modelling the quality of
service that surviving links provide.  When end-to-end latency is
addressed at all, it is either absent from the cascade dynamics or
applied as a post-hoc rescaling of the final performance metric, so
that all delay scenarios share an identical structural cascade
\citep{Milano2012,LaiCPPS2019}.  More recent work has begun to
relax this binary view by modelling a single delay channel
explicitly---typically queueing on transmission links or jitter on
PMU streams---and embedding it inside the cascade
\citep{Cai2016TSG,Falahati2014TSG,Huang2015}.  This direction is the
right one, but the chain that feeds modern wide-area protection is
not single-channel.  Industrial measurement budgets distinguish
sensor and PMU acquisition, edge processing, transmission and
actuation as separately bounded components
\citep{NASPI_LatencyGuidelines,IEEE_C37_118_2}, and the redundancy
practices that sit on top of them
\citep{IEC_61850_PRP} suppress the loss probability of each
component to very different degrees.  A model in which all of these
components collapse into a scalar~$\tau$ therefore cannot, by
construction, reproduce the asymmetric way real cyber stress maps
into closed-loop control degradation.

This observation motivates the central research question of the
present paper:
\begin{quote}
\emph{Does embedding a standard-anchored, multi-component
communication delay model inside every round of a CPPS cascade
produce qualitatively different vulnerability conclusions from a
delay-free or single-$\tau$ model on the same network?}
\end{quote}

We answer this question affirmatively, on three counts that we
develop in the body of the paper: the delay penalty is asymmetrically
concentrated at moderate-to-high tolerance margins (precisely the
operating regime in which a delay-free analysis predicts safety);
delay amplifies the stochastic variability of cascade outcomes,
producing heavy-tailed terminal distributions that ideal-communication
models do not exhibit; and a generator-fidelity metric exposes
functional degradation that aggregate load-retention metrics fail to
detect.  The mechanism that produces all three effects is a
self-reinforcing loop: degraded communication reduces the closed-loop
control efficacy of every surviving generator, the resulting
imbalance triggers additional branch overloads, the structural
failures further damage the cyber topology, and the damaged topology
amplifies the latency that drives the next round.  Our prior work
\citep{ChenTu2026} established the underlying CPPS framework with
distributed control centers, a Functional Viability-Constrained
Island Detection mechanism and a Cohesion-Propagation Importance
Metric for control-center placement, but did not model the
influence of communication delay on generator control during the
cascade itself.  The present paper closes that gap.

The contributions of this work are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{A delay-integrated, closed-loop cascading failure
  framework.}  Communication delay is computed inside every cascade
  round and applied to generator output \emph{before} the
  DC power-flow redistribution that determines branch overloads.
  Each delay scenario therefore evolves along a genuinely
  different trajectory---different overload patterns, different
  failure sequences, different terminal states---rather than along
  a rescaled copy of a common delay-free trajectory.  The same
  loop also drives an underfrequency load-shedding (UFLS) rule
  whose shed depth is a function of the prevailing latency, so
  that the standard NERC PRC-006-5 single-stage rule is recovered
  as the zero-delay limit.

\item \textbf{A four-component, role-aware end-to-end delay
  decomposition.}  Latency is split into measurement
  ($\tau_m$), edge processing ($\tau_e$), transmission ($\tau_t$)
  and actuation ($\tau_a$) components, with role-dependent service
  times for control centers and ordinary relay nodes, mirroring
  the asymmetric processing behaviour of SCADA / EMS architectures.
  Each component is parameterised against an externally defensible
  reference: NASPI synchrophasor latency
  guidelines~\citep{NASPI_LatencyGuidelines} and
  IEEE~C37.118.2~\citep{IEEE_C37_118_2} bound $\tau_m$ and
  $\tau_t$, the IEC~61850 PRP / HSR redundancy
  stack~\citep{IEC_61850_PRP} bounds the residual loss
  probability, and the NERC PRC-002-2 sub-second budget
  \citep{NERC_PRC002} bounds the heaviest scenario we test.

\item \textbf{A three-mechanism control-efficacy function
  $\boldsymbol{\eta^{+} = \Phi_{\mathrm{loss}} \cdot
  \Phi_{\mathrm{sat}} \cdot \Phi_{\mathrm{crit}}}$.}  Delay-induced
  generator-control loss is decomposed into three orthogonal
  mechanisms with separately tunable parameters:
  redundancy-attenuated message loss
  ($\Phi_{\mathrm{loss}}$), continuous controller saturation under
  elevated $\tau_m+\tau_e$ ($\Phi_{\mathrm{sat}}$) and a normalised
  collapse near each machine's critical
  delay~$\tau_{\mathrm{crit},i}$ ($\Phi_{\mathrm{crit}}$, normalised
  so that $\eta^{+}(\tau{=}0) = 1$ exactly).  Because the three
  mechanisms are orthogonal in parameter space, any observed
  vulnerability can be attributed to a specific physical channel,
  rather than absorbed into a single black-box delay penalty.

\item \textbf{Twin resilience metrics that reveal the asymmetric
  delay-penalty regime and its hidden component.}  The cascade
  is evaluated jointly on a supply-conservation ratio~$R_1$ and on
  a capacity-weighted dispatch-fidelity NRMSE~$R_3$.  A
  delay-penalty heatmap on the (tolerance, cascade-round) plane
  shows that the damage is largest at moderate-to-high tolerance
  margins, narrowing the critical intervention window to the
  early cascade rounds, while $R_3$ exposes generator-level
  functional degradation that $R_1$ alone misses.  These results
  show that delay-free and single-$\tau$ robustness models are
  not merely imprecise but systematically misleading in the
  operating regime that operators most often rely on.

\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:model} presents the CPPS model, including the
four-component communication delay formulation and the
betweenness-assortative interdependent coupling.
Section~\ref{sec:cascade} describes the delay-integrated cascading
failure dynamics, the $\eta^{+}$ control-efficacy function, the
delay-coupled UFLS rule and the five delay-severity scenarios.
Section~\ref{sec:experiment} reports Monte~Carlo experiments on the
IEEE~39-bus test system coupled to a Barab\'asi--Albert scale-free
communication network, covering 1\,000 coupling realisations,
eleven tolerance levels and the five delay scenarios, and analyses
the asymmetric delay-penalty regime, the heavy-tailed collapse
distributions, the hidden $R_3$ degradation, the round-level
intervention window and a sensitivity analysis of mitigation
actions.  Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

\section{Cyber-Physical Power System Model}\label{sec:model}

The system under study consists of two interdependent network layers,
a physical power grid~$G_P$ and a cyber communication network~$G_C$,
connected by a one-to-one interlayer map.  Sections~\ref{sec:power}
and~\ref{sec:cyber} define the two layers individually,
Section~\ref{sec:coupling} defines the map, and
Section~\ref{sec:capacity} defines the single tolerance parameter
$\alpha$ that is swept in every experiment of
Section~\ref{sec:experiment}.  The level of detail in this section is
deliberately limited to what is required to support the closed-loop
cascade dynamics of Section~\ref{sec:cascade}; mechanism-specific
detail (the $\eta^{+}$ control-efficacy function, the delay-coupled
under-frequency load-shedding rule and the five severity scenarios)
is collected in Section~\ref{sec:cascade}.

%% ---- 2.1 Power Grid Layer -------------------------------------------
\subsection{Physical power layer}\label{sec:power}

The physical layer is the IEEE~39-bus (New England) test system, a
benchmark comprising $|V_P|=39$ buses, 46~transmission branches and
10~generators that has been used as a reference test bed for
cascading-failure analysis since the work of \citet{Athay1979}.  The
nodal admittance and branch parameters are taken from the
MATPOWER~7.1 case file \citep{Zimmerman2011matpower}; the initial
operating point---bus loads $P_d^{(0)}$, generator dispatch
$P_g^{(0)}$ and branch flows $P_e^{(0)}$---is obtained by solving a
DC power flow on the full network.

Under the DC assumption the active-power balance is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dcpf}
  \mathbf{P} \;=\; \mathbf{B}\,\boldsymbol{\Theta},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{P}=[p_1,\dots,p_{|V_P|}]^{\!\top}$ stacks nodal
injections ($p_i>0$ for generation, $p_i<0$ for load),
$\boldsymbol{\Theta}$ is the bus voltage-angle vector and
$\mathbf{B}$ is the bus susceptance matrix with
$B_{ij}=-b_{ij}$ for $i\ne j$ and
$B_{ii}=\sum_{k\ne i}b_{ik}$, $b_{ij}=1/x_{ij}$.  The DC formulation is
adopted for two reasons: it is the de~facto standard in cascading-failure
literature \citep{Dobson2007,Brummitt2012}, and its linearity admits
the analytical separation of delay-induced injection changes from
post-redistribution branch flows that we will require in
Section~\ref{sec:cascade}.  Reactive power, voltage magnitudes and
transient stability are outside the scope of the present model; the
implications for ancillary protections are discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

%% ---- 2.2 Communication Network Layer --------------------------------
\subsection{Cyber communication layer}\label{sec:cyber}

The cyber layer carries every measurement and control packet on which
the closed-loop dispatch of Section~\ref{sec:cascade} depends.  It is
specified by three ingredients: a topology
(Section~\ref{sec:topology}), a placement rule for the control
centres that originate dispatch commands (Section~\ref{sec:msis})
and an end-to-end delay model that converts the topology into a
per-generator latency budget (Section~\ref{sec:delay_model}).

\subsubsection{Scale-free topology and sizing}\label{sec:topology}

The cyber topology is generated by the Barab\'asi--Albert (BA)
preferential-attachment process \citep{BarabasiAlbert1999}, which
reproduces the heavy-tailed degree distribution and hub--spoke
asymmetry empirically reported for utility-grade communication
backbones \citep{Pagani2013}.  The network has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cyber_size}
  |V_C| \;=\; |V_P| + n_{\text{cc}},
  \qquad
  n_{\text{cc}} \;=\; \lceil 0.2\,|V_P|\rceil,
\end{equation}
nodes; the additional $n_{\text{cc}}$ nodes are designated control
centres (CCs) and the remaining $|V_P|$ nodes are non-CC relay nodes
that map one-to-one onto power buses (Section~\ref{sec:coupling}).
For the IEEE~39-bus test bed this yields $|V_C|=47$ nodes of which
$n_{\text{cc}}=8$ are CCs.  The 0.2 ratio is consistent with the
practice of provisioning roughly one regional dispatch centre per
five substation-class nodes reported in
\citep{NASPI_LatencyGuidelines}.

The BA growth process is initialised from a fully connected seed of
$m_0=4$ nodes; at each step a new node is attached to $m_e=2$
existing nodes with probability proportional to their current
degree.  Because each Monte Carlo trial of
Section~\ref{sec:experiment} regenerates $G_C$ from a fresh random
seed, the reported metrics average out the stochasticity of
individual realisations.

\subsubsection{Control-centre placement via a multi-attribute
structural importance score}\label{sec:msis}

Of the $|V_C|$ cyber nodes generated by~\eqref{eq:cyber_size},
$n_{\text{cc}}$ must be promoted to control-centre status.  A choice
that minimises the expected end-to-end delay between any non-CC node
and its nearest surviving CC is desirable, but no single centrality
measure captures all the structural attributes that matter under
cascading stress: betweenness identifies bridging nodes whose removal
fragments the network; closeness identifies nodes with short
expected distances to the rest of the graph; the $k$-shell index
identifies nodes embedded in dense cores that resist localised
attack; degree and clustering identify nodes whose immediate
neighbourhood is itself well-connected.  Each of these is necessary
but none is sufficient.  We therefore adopt a multi-attribute
structural importance score (MSIS) that fuses them
multiplicatively/additively:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:msis}
  w_i \;=\; \frac{\widetilde{B}(i)+K_s(i)}{C_l(i)} \;+\; D(i)\cdot C(i),
\end{equation}
where $\widetilde{B}(i)=B(i)+1$ is the (shifted) node-betweenness
centrality of node~$i$ in $G_C$, $K_s(i)$ its $k$-shell index,
$C_l(i)$ its closeness centrality, $D(i)$ its degree and $C(i)$ its
local clustering coefficient.  The shift $+1$ in $\widetilde{B}$
prevents the leaf nodes ($B=0$) from being absorbed into the second
term alone, which would degenerate the score for tree-like
sub-graphs.  The $n_{\text{cc}}$ nodes with the largest $w_i$ are
designated as CCs; ties are broken by node index to preserve
reproducibility.

The first term, $(\widetilde{B}+K_s)/C_l$, rewards a node that is
\emph{simultaneously} a bridging point (high $\widetilde{B}$) and
core-embedded (high $K_s$), while penalising nodes that are
peripheral in the average-distance sense (large $C_l$, i.e.\ far from
the rest of the graph).\footnote{The form
$(\widetilde{B}+K_s)/C_l$ rather than $(\widetilde{B}+K_s)\cdot
(1/C_l)$ is used so that the dimensional scale of $\widetilde{B}+K_s$
is not crushed by the small numerical range of $1/C_l$ in dense
sub-graphs; on $G_C$ with $|V_C|=47$ the two terms then enter
$w_i$ with comparable order of magnitude.}  The second term,
$D\cdot C$, rewards a node whose own neighbourhood is locally
cohesive, which has been shown to correlate with the survival of
short alternative paths after a single-node attack
\citep{Fortunato2010,Schneider2011}.  The composite~$w_i$ therefore
balances global bridging value against local resilience, the two
properties that we will need in Section~\ref{sec:cascade} when CCs
are required to remain reachable from a fragmenting cyber graph.

A multi-attribute control-centre placement metric in the same spirit
was proposed independently in our companion study \citep{ChenTu2026}
under the name CPIM (Cohesion-Propagation Importance Metric); MSIS
adopts the same multi-attribute philosophy but replaces CPIM's
neighbour-distance summation by the closeness denominator
in~\eqref{eq:msis} and by the explicit $D\cdot C$ term, both of which
are computed in $\mathcal{O}(|V_C|^2)$ rather than $\mathcal{O}(|V_C|^3)$
and therefore scale to the per-trial regeneration of~$G_C$ used in
this paper.  Because \citep{ChenTu2026} is still under review,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:msis} is given here in full so that the present paper
is self-contained.

\subsubsection{Four-component, role-aware end-to-end delay
model}\label{sec:delay_model}

The end-to-end latency between a power bus~$b$ and a control
centre~$c$ is, in industrial practice, the sum of four bounded
components: a measurement-acquisition term~$\tau_m$ at the field
side; an edge-processing term~$\tau_e$ inside the substation
gateway; a transmission term~$\tau_t$ along the cyber path; and an
actuation term~$\tau_a$ at the receiving plant
\citep{NASPI_LatencyGuidelines,IEEE_C37_118_2}.  Our delay model
makes each of these four components individually computable, with
role-dependent service times for CC and non-CC nodes that mirror the
processing asymmetry of SCADA/EMS architectures
\citep{Kundur2017CPPS}.

\paragraph{Per-link transmission delay.}
For a single surviving cyber link $(u,v)$, the per-hop transmission
delay is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:link_delay}
  T_{\text{link}}(u,v) \;=\; \frac{S}{R} \;+\; \frac{d_{uv}}{V},
\end{equation}
where $S$ is the packet size in bits, $R$ is the link data rate, $d_{uv}$
is the effective fibre distance and $V$ is the propagation speed.  The
first term is serialisation, the second is propagation.  Uplink and
downlink traffic carry different packet sizes---measurement frames
are larger than command frames, $S_{\text{up}}=1024\,\text{B}$ vs.\
$S_{\text{down}}=256\,\text{B}$ in our base configuration---so the
same physical link contributes asymmetrically to the two directions
even before any role effects are added.  The reference link
parameters used throughout this paper, $R=10\,\text{Mbps}$ and
$V=2{\times}10^{5}\,\text{km/s}$, are within the IEEE C37.118.2
typical-installation band \citep{IEEE_C37_118_2}.

\paragraph{Per-hop edge-processing delay.}
At each intermediate cyber node~$u$ along a path, a forwarding
service delay~$\tau_{\text{fwd}}(u)$ is incurred:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fwd}
  \tau_{\text{fwd}}(u) \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    \tau_{\text{CC}}^{\text{fwd}},   & u\text{ is a CC},\\[2pt]
    \tau_{\text{nonCC}}^{\text{fwd}},& \text{otherwise}.
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Control centres are equipped with carrier-grade routing hardware
and therefore exhibit a substantially lower per-packet service
latency than ordinary substation gateways; this is the source of
the role-awareness that distinguishes our model from a single-rate
queueing fabric.  The same role distinction applies to the
endpoint contributions $\tau^{\text{tx}}$ and $\tau^{\text{rx}}$ at
the originating and terminating nodes.  Reference values used in
the simulations are reported in Table~\ref{tab:service_times};
they place CC service times at one quarter of the corresponding
non-CC times, consistent with the order-of-magnitude gap between
EMS-grade routers and substation IEDs reported by
\citep{NASPI_LatencyGuidelines}.

\paragraph{Path-level aggregation.}
For a multi-hop path $\pi=(u_0,u_1,\dots,u_k)$ in $G_C$ between a
non-CC source and a CC, or vice versa, the total path delay sums
the link, endpoint and forwarding contributions.  Writing the
intermediate nodes as $u_1,\dots,u_{k-1}$,
\begin{align}
  T_{\text{down}}(\pi) &=
    \sum_{i=0}^{k-1}T_{\text{link}}^{\text{down}}(u_i,u_{i+1})
    + \tau_{\text{CC}}^{\text{tx}} + \tau_{\text{nonCC}}^{\text{rx}}
    + \!\!\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\!\tau_{\text{fwd}}(u_j),
    \label{eq:Tdown}\\[2pt]
  T_{\text{up}}(\pi) &=
    \sum_{i=0}^{k-1}T_{\text{link}}^{\text{up}}(u_i,u_{i+1})
    + \tau_{\text{nonCC}}^{\text{tx}} + \tau_{\text{CC}}^{\text{rx}}
    + \!\!\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\!\tau_{\text{fwd}}(u_j),
    \label{eq:Tup}
\end{align}
for the downlink (CC$\to$non-CC) and uplink (non-CC$\to$CC)
directions respectively.  The same physical path therefore yields a
different uplink and downlink delay because of (a) directional
packet-size asymmetry in $T_{\text{link}}$, (b) the directional
endpoint terms ($\tau_{\text{CC}}^{\text{tx}}$ vs.\
$\tau_{\text{nonCC}}^{\text{tx}}$), and (c) role-dependent
$\tau_{\text{fwd}}$ at intermediate nodes; not because of any
asymmetric propagation physics on the link itself.

\paragraph{Mapping to the four-component decomposition.}
The four components named in Contribution~2 of the Introduction
correspond to the quantities defined above as follows.  The
transmission term~$\tau_t$ is the path-level link sum
$\sum_i T_{\text{link}}(u_i,u_{i+1})$.  The edge-processing
term~$\tau_e$ is the path-level forwarding sum
$\sum_j \tau_{\text{fwd}}(u_j)$ together with the appropriate
endpoint receive contribution.  The measurement
term~$\tau_m$ aggregates the field-side acquisition delay
$\tau_{\text{PB}\to\text{nonCC}}$ at the substation interface with the
uplink path delay $T_{\text{up}}(\pi_i)$ feeding the nearest CC.
The actuation term~$\tau_a$ aggregates the downlink path delay
$T_{\text{down}}(\pi_i)$ from the controlling CC with the field-side
execution delay $\tau_{\text{nonCC}\to\text{PB}}$.  The two field-side
contributions, set to $0.10\,\text{s}$ and $0.12\,\text{s}$
respectively in the \texttt{baseline} scenario, are anchored against
the round-trip SCADA RTU acquisition+execution budget reported in
\citep{NASPI_LatencyGuidelines} and are scaled jointly with the
cyber service times under each severity scenario of
Section~\ref{sec:scenarios}.  The five scenarios collectively span
the range from an ideal channel ($\tau_m+\tau_e=0$) to the
\texttt{heavy} regime
($\tau_m+\tau_e\!\approx\!594\,\text{ms}$, ${\sim}60\%$ of the
NERC PRC-002-2 sub-second event-reporting upper bound
\citep{NERC_PRC002}); see Section~\ref{sec:scenarios} for the full
parameterisation.

\paragraph{Why four components and not a single $\tau$.}
A model in which $\tau_m$, $\tau_e$, $\tau_t$ and $\tau_a$ collapse
into a single scalar latency cannot, by construction, distinguish
a long-distance fibre regime ($\tau_t$ dominates, $\tau_e$ small,
loss probability low) from a congested edge regime ($\tau_e$
dominates, $\tau_t$ small, loss probability high).  Yet these two
regimes have markedly different consequences for closed-loop
control, because the loss probability per hop scales with $\tau_e$
through the queueing-induced congestion term of
Section~\ref{sec:cascade} and not with $\tau_t$.  Carrying the four
components separately is therefore the minimum that lets the
control-efficacy function $\eta^{+}$ in Section~\ref{sec:cascade}
attribute observed vulnerability to a specific physical channel.

%% ---- 2.3 Interdependent Coupling ------------------------------------
\subsection{Interdependent coupling}\label{sec:coupling}

The two layers are coupled through a one-to-one map between power
buses and non-CC cyber nodes, encoded in a binary coupling matrix
$A_{pc}\in\{0,1\}^{|V_P|\times(|V_C|-n_{\text{cc}})}$ such that
$A_{pc}(b,n)=1$ when bus $b\in V_P$ is monitored by non-CC node
$n\in V_C\setminus\mathcal{C}$ and zero otherwise, where
$\mathcal{C}$ denotes the set of $n_{\text{cc}}$ control centres.
Control centres are not coupled to any power bus directly; they
exchange traffic with the field only via the substation gateways
they control, in line with the layered architecture mandated by
NERC CIP-012 \citep{NERC_CIP012}.

\paragraph{Betweenness-assortative pairing.}
$A_{pc}$ is constructed by sorting the power buses in descending
order of their betweenness centrality in $G_P$, independently
sorting the non-CC cyber nodes in descending order of their
betweenness centrality in $G_C$, and pairing them rank by rank.
The rationale is operational: structurally critical buses---those
whose removal would fragment the largest number of source--sink
paths in the power graph---are the buses for which a utility
provisions the most capable monitoring and the highest-priority
communication channels, and these are correspondingly placed on
the structurally critical communication nodes.  Random or
disassortative coupling rules are also defensible in principle,
but they describe networks that have not been engineered with
operational priority in mind and would therefore systematically
overstate the cyber-physical fragility of a real grid.

\paragraph{Bidirectionality of the coupled channel.}
Crucially, the coupling map is used twice in every cascade round:
(i)~upwards, to deliver the field-side measurement of bus~$b$ along
the uplink path~$\pi_b$ to the nearest surviving CC, contributing
to $\tau_m$ via Eq.~\eqref{eq:Tup}; and (ii)~downwards, to deliver
the dispatch command from that CC back to the substation gateway
for execution at bus~$b$, contributing to $\tau_a$ via
Eq.~\eqref{eq:Tdown}.  The single coupling map therefore carries
both directions of the closed-loop control channel, which is
precisely what allows a structural failure in $G_C$ to lengthen
\emph{both} $\tau_m$ and $\tau_a$ for a given generator and
thereby couple cyber-side damage into the power-side dispatch
within the same cascade round.

\paragraph{Scope of the contribution claimed.}
The pairing rule above is a re-statement of standard practice in
the interdependent-networks literature
\citep{Buldyrev2010,Brummitt2012,ChenTu2026} and is not itself
claimed as a contribution of the present paper.  What is novel,
and what is set up in Section~\ref{sec:cascade}, is the use of
this same map to carry the four-component delay budget into the
generator dispatch \emph{before} the DC power-flow redistribution
of each cascade round, so that the structural state of $G_C$
modulates the electrical state of $G_P$ in the same iteration.

%% ---- 2.4 Capacity-Tolerance Model -----------------------------------
\subsection{Capacity--tolerance model}\label{sec:capacity}

Overload-driven cascading requires a model of component capacity.
We adopt the standard tolerance-factor formulation of
\citet{Motter2002}, applied symmetrically to power branches, cyber
nodes and cyber edges:
\begin{align}
  C_e^{P}   &= (1+\alpha)\,\bigl|P_e^{(0)}\bigr|, &
  e&\in E_P, \label{eq:cap_P}\\
  C_i^{C}   &= (1+\alpha)\,B_i^{(0)}, &
  i&\in V_C, \label{eq:cap_Cn}\\
  C_e^{CE}  &= (1+\alpha)\,B_e^{(0)}, &
  e&\in E_C, \label{eq:cap_Ce}
\end{align}
where $P_e^{(0)}$ is the initial DC-PF flow on power branch~$e$,
$B_i^{(0)}$ the initial node-betweenness of cyber node~$i$ and
$B_e^{(0)}$ the initial edge-betweenness of cyber link~$e$.

The single tolerance parameter $\alpha\in[0,1]$ governs the excess
capacity of every component: at $\alpha=0$ each component operates
at exactly its initial load and any redistribution will trip it;
at $\alpha=1$ each component can absorb up to twice its initial
load before tripping.  A common $\alpha$ across the two layers is
adopted because the two layers are typically built and reinforced
to a common operational margin, and because using two independent
tolerances would expand the experimental design from a one-dimensional
sweep to a two-dimensional surface for no clear physical
gain---existing CPPS literature is essentially unanimous on this
choice \citep{Motter2002,Buldyrev2010,Cai2016TSG}.  Throughout the
experiments of Section~\ref{sec:experiment}, $\alpha$ is swept in
increments of~0.1 over the closed interval $[0,1]$, giving the
eleven tolerance levels reported there.

A subtle point is that, because $C_i^{C}$ and $C_e^{CE}$ depend on
the \emph{initial} betweenness while overload is checked against
the \emph{updated} betweenness on the surviving cyber graph, a node
or edge whose role becomes more central after a structural failure
will see its load grow against a frozen capacity and may then
overload---this is the cyber-side analogue of the power-side
flow-redistribution overload of \citet{Motter2002} and is the
mechanism through which a single targeted cyber attack can fan out
into a multi-component cyber cascade in Section~\ref{sec:cascade}.
